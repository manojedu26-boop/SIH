1. Executive Summary
The Members of Parliament Local Area Development Scheme (MPLADS) is a
₹5-crore-per-MP, per-year Central Sector Scheme through which elected representatives
recommend local infrastructure works — drinking water, sanitation, roads, schools, health
facilities — executed by District Authorities. It moves roughly ₹3,800–4,000 crore of public
money every year across 786+ MPs (Lok Sabha + Rajya Sabha), yet it has been repeatedly
flagged by the Comptroller and Auditor General (CAG) for poor monitoring, delayed
execution, and diversion of funds to ineligible or duplicate works.

SIH26102 asks for an AI system that ingests MPLADS project and expenditure data, detects
suspicious spending patterns, duplicate/ghost projects, chronic delays, and statistical
outliers, assigns each project a risk score, and surfaces everything through an
investigator-facing dashboard. This report gives a complete technical and strategic blueprint:
problem framing, prior art, data landscape, system architecture, the specific
anomaly-detection and fraud-detection methods to use (and why), the risk-scoring formula,
the dashboard design, a tech stack, a build roadmap, feasibility analysis, and the pitch-level
differentiators that will matter to judges.

   ●​ Real, well-documented pain point: CAG audits and multiple RTI-based
      investigations have repeatedly found under-utilization, delayed completion, and

   ●​ Public data exists: Unlike many "detect fraud" hackathon prompts that quietly

3. Background: What the MPLAD Scheme Actually Is
Understanding the scheme's real mechanics is essential — you will be asked pointed
questions by judges about domain understanding, not just the model.

   ●​ Origin: Launched 23 December 1993 as a Central Sector Scheme; moved from the
      Ministry of Rural Development to the Ministry of Statistics and Programme
      Implementation (MoSPI) in 1994, which has administered it since.
   ●​ Purpose: Enables each MP to recommend durable community assets — drinking
      water, sanitation, primary education infrastructure, public health, roads, electricity —
      based on locally felt needs, in sectors and quantities they choose within the
      guidelines.
   ●​ Fund flow: each MP is allocated ₹5 crore per year (since 2011-12), released by
      MoSPI to district authorities in two installments of ₹2.5 crore. This fund is
      non-lapsable and can be carried forward if not utilised in a given year.
   ●​ Who can recommend what: Lok Sabha MPs recommend works in their own
      constituency; Rajya Sabha MPs recommend works anywhere in their state of
      election; nominated MPs can recommend works anywhere in the country — this
      cross-district flexibility for RS/nominated MPs is itself a place where
      duplicate/overlapping recommendations become possible and worth modeling.
   ●​ Mandatory social allocation: MPs must allocate a minimum of 15% and 7.5% of
      their funds to create assets for Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes respectively
      — a rule your system should explicitly check compliance against per MP per year (an
      easy, high-value, low-effort feature).
   ●​ Role separation (important for fraud modeling): The MP's role stops at
      recommending a work. Sanctioning, execution and completion within the stipulated
      time is the responsibility of the District Authority, and the exact expenditure figures
      are available only with the District Authorities — meaning your fraud signals should
      be architected around District Authority + Implementing Agency behavior, not
      just "which MP is corrupt," since that's where execution-stage fraud (inflated billing,
      fictitious completion, vendor collusion) actually happens.
   ●​ Digitization milestone: the MPLADS–eSAKSHI web portal (mplads.mospi.gov.in)
      was introduced from 1 April 2023 to manage the revised fund-flow procedure, with a
      revamped public dashboard showing works recommended, works sanctioned, works
      completed, and expenditure on completed and ongoing works, with drill-down tabs.
      Before that, the scheme ran in physical mode and district-wise work data was
      maintained locally by District Authorities — meaning pre-2023 data is
      fragmented/inconsistent, and this is a genuine data-quality problem your report
      should acknowledge rather than gloss over.
   ●​ A known suspension event: the scheme was suspended for two years
      (2020–2022) to fund COVID-19 response and restored from 1 April 2022 — your
      time-series anomaly models need to know about this gap so they don't flag it as
      "fraud."
   ●​ Documented misuse pattern (cite this in your problem-justification slide): funds
      have been diverted for ineligible projects, sometimes transferred to private trusts
      controlled by MPs, execution handed to favoured contractors, with no effective
      mechanism to monitor expenditure, and the CAG has repeatedly made unfavourable
      observations on the scheme.
   ●​ What MPLADS funds may not be used for: personal benefit, private institutions,
      religious structures, land acquisition, or routine maintenance work — this gives you a
      concrete, checkable eligibility ruleset to encode as rule-based fraud flags (a project
      categorized under an ineligible purpose is an instant hard flag, no ML required).




4. Data Landscape — What You Can Actually Use
This is the section most teams skip, and it's the one judges probe hardest ("where does your
data come from, is it real").
4.1 Primary real sources

   1.​ MPLADS eSAKSHI public dashboard (mplads.mospi.gov.in) — works
       recommended, sanctioned, completed, and expenditure figures, with drill-down for
       detail, current from FY2023-24 onward.
   2.​ data.gov.in / Dataful.in mirrors — structured historical datasets, e.g. state-,
       constituency- and MP-wise entitlement/release/sanction/expenditure figures
       spanning the 12th to 17th Lok Sabhas, and
       year-/state-/district-/constituency-/MP-wise lists of sanctioned works — these are the
       most hackathon-usable, clean, tabular datasets available and should be your primary
       training/demo source.
   3.​ PIB (Press Information Bureau) releases — periodic ministry statements with
       state-wise pending-works tables, useful for validating your delay-detection outputs
       against officially acknowledged backlogs.
   4.​ CAG audit reports — not a training dataset, but a goldmine of known, confirmed
       fraud cases you can use as a labeled "ground truth" test set to validate that your
       anomaly detector actually flags real historical irregularities (this is a very strong thing
       to show judges: "here is a case CAG confirmed was fraudulent, and our system flags
       it at risk score 87/100").

4.2 Known data-quality constraints (state these proactively, don't hide
them)

   ●​ Pre-2023-24 data for Rajya Sabha MPs and pre-2023-24 recommended/sanctioned
      work-level detail is largely unavailable on eSAKSHI, since the scheme ran in
      physical/manual mode before then.
   ●​ No scheme-wide unique project ID standard prior to digitization — duplicate detection
      pre-2023 must rely on fuzzy matching, not exact-ID joins.
   ●​ Geolocation is only available at district/constituency granularity in most public
      datasets, not GPS-level per project — your geospatial layer should be designed
      around district/constituency polygons, not point coordinates (unless you supplement
      with a manually geocoded sample for the demo).

4.3 Recommended demo data strategy

Build the pipeline generically, but for the hackathon demo:

   ●​ Use the real Dataful/data.gov.in tabular datasets for 3–5 states as the "real data"
      backbone (this is your credibility anchor).
   ●​ Synthetically inject a small number of clearly-labeled fraud patterns (duplicate project
      descriptions across districts, a district with 95% fund utilization but 0% physical
      completion, a contractor appearing across an implausible number of unrelated
      districts) so you can demonstrate detection live, and clearly disclose in the demo
      which records are real vs. injected for demonstration.
5. Existing Landscape / Prior Art
  ●​ eSAKSHI dashboard itself: already shows aggregated
     recommended/sanctioned/completed/expenditure figures, but it is a reporting tool,
     not a detection tool — it shows you the numbers, it does not tell you which numbers
     are suspicious. This is your clearest differentiation line for the pitch: "eSAKSHI tells
     you what happened, we tell you what's wrong."
  ●​ CAG audits: manual, sample-based, retrospective (often years after the fact) — your
     system is continuous and near-real-time by contrast.
  ●​ General GovTech fraud-analytics academic literature: procurement fraud
     detection commonly uses Benford's Law on invoice amounts, graph analysis on
     vendor-official relationships, and NLP similarity on tender descriptions — all directly
     transferable to MPLADS work descriptions and vendor/contractor data.
  ●​ Gap you are filling: no existing public tool combines (a) fund-flow anomaly
     detection, (b) duplicate/ghost-project detection via NLP+geo, and (c) an
     investigator-facing explainable risk score, specifically for MPLADS. That combination
     is your unique-value-proposition sentence.




6. Objectives
  1.​ Ingest multi-year, multi-state MPLADS project and expenditure records into a unified
      schema.
  2.​ Detect four categories of irregularity: financial anomalies, duplicate/near-duplicate
      projects, execution delays, and statistical/behavioral outliers.
  3.​ Produce a single, explainable 0–100 risk score per project (and roll it up to per-MP
      and per-district scores).
  4.​ Present findings via a dual-audience dashboard: a public transparency view and a
      restricted investigator view with case-flagging and audit-trail export.
  5.​ Keep every flag explainable — no black-box scores; every flag must show why
      (which rule or model feature fired).

8. Detection Methodology — the Technical Core
This is the part judges will scrutinize most. Below is method-by-method reasoning, not just a
list of buzzwords.

8.1 Financial / spending anomaly detection
  ●​ Benford's Law check on expenditure amounts per district/vendor — a classic,
     cheap, explainable first-pass fraud signal (real, organic financial data follows a
     predictable leading-digit distribution; manipulated figures usually don't).
  ●​ Z-score / IQR outlier detection on per-work cost relative to the median cost of
     similar-category works (e.g., a "hand pump installation" costing 8x the state median
     for that category is an instant flag) — simple, explainable, good baseline before you
     reach for ML.
  ●​ Isolation Forest / Local Outlier Factor on a multivariate feature vector per project
     (cost, category, district, time-to-completion, utilization %) to catch anomalies that
     univariate rules miss.
  ●​ Utilization-rate anomalies: districts/MPs with unusually high "funds released" but
     unusually low "physical completion %" — a strong, intuitive proxy for either fund
     parking or non-execution.
  ●​ SC/ST allocation compliance check (rule-based, not ML): flag any MP-year where
     the mandatory 15%/7.5% SC/ST allocation was not met — this is a directly auditable,
     hard compliance rule and gives you a guaranteed-accurate flag category to show in
     the demo (no model uncertainty involved).

8.2 Duplicate / ghost project detection

  ●​ Text similarity: embed work descriptions (e.g., using sentence-transformers) and
     compute cosine similarity across projects within the same district/year to catch
     near-identical works recommended/sanctioned more than once (a classic MPLADS
     fraud pattern: the same "community hall" appearing under slightly reworded
     descriptions in consecutive years).
  ●​ Geospatial clustering: cluster projects by district/constituency + category +
     approximate location; a cluster with implausibly many "distinct" works of the same
     type in the same small area is a red flag for either genuine oversupply or duplicate
     billing.
  ●​ Cost-per-unit consistency check: for standardized asset types (e.g., cost per meter
     of road, cost per handpump), compare against a state-level benchmark; large
     deviations combined with high text-similarity to another project is a strong
     duplicate-fraud composite signal.

8.3 Delay / execution-inefficiency detection

  ●​ Guideline benchmark: MPLADS guidelines (paras 3.2.4, 3.2.12, 10.6.1) require
     completion of projects in a time-bound manner, and industry practice cited in scheme
     documentation is roughly one year from sanction — model "days since sanction with
     status ≠ completed" against this benchmark as your core delay metric.
  ●​ Track chronic-delay districts/agencies — implementing agencies whose average
     completion time is a statistical outlier vs. peers doing the same category of work.
  ●​ Use survival-analysis style modeling (e.g., time-to-completion curves per category)
     so delay flags account for the fact that some categories (e.g., large road works) are
     legitimately slower than others (e.g., hand pumps) — this avoids naive false
     positives.
8.4 Vendor / Implementing Agency graph analysis (high-value
differentiator)

   ●​ Build a graph: District Authority → Implementing Agency → Vendor/Contractor.
   ●​ Flag agencies/vendors with anomalous concentration (one contractor executing an
      implausible share of works across unrelated districts), or with sanction-to-payment
      timing patterns inconsistent with genuine execution.
   ●​ This directly targets the CAG-documented pattern of execution being handed to
      favoured agencies and contractors.




9. Risk Scoring Model
Design principle: explainable and auditable over accurate-but-opaque. Investigators
need to defend a flag in a review, so a black-box neural score alone is a liability, not a
feature.

Composite score (0–100) = weighted sum of normalized sub-scores:

      Component            Weigh                             Source
                             t

 Financial anomaly         30%      Benford deviation + cost-outlier z-score +
 sub-score                          utilization-mismatch

 Duplicate/ghost-projec    25%      Text-similarity max + geo-cluster density
 t sub-score

 Delay sub-score           20%      Days-overdue vs. category benchmark

 Compliance sub-score      15%      SC/ST allocation breach, ineligible-category flag
                                    (rule-based, binary/hard flags contribute heavily here)

 Vendor/agency graph       10%      Concentration/anomaly score from the agency-vendor
 sub-score                          graph

Each sub-score above a threshold contributes a human-readable reason string ("Cost is
6.2x the state median for this work category," "92% text-similarity to Project #4471
sanctioned 14 months earlier in the same district") which is what actually shows up on the
investigator's card — the number is secondary to the explanation.

Roll-up scores: project-level → MP-level (average + max of their projects) →
district/state-level (aggregate risk heatmap) so the dashboard supports drilling both up and
down.
10. Dashboard Design
Two audiences, two views, one data backbone:

   1.​ Public Transparency View (no login)
          ○​ Choropleth map of India, shaded by state/constituency fund-utilization and
              aggregate risk.
          ○​ Search by MP/constituency → shows recommended vs. sanctioned vs.
              completed works, expenditure timeline, and a plain-language "transparency
              score."
          ○​ No individual "fraud" accusation language shown publicly — frame it as
              "review-recommended" or "data inconsistency flagged," to stay legally
              responsible in the pitch narrative (judges will respect this nuance).
   2.​ Investigator / Audit View (auth-gated, role-based)
          ○​ Ranked queue of flagged projects by risk score, filterable by
              state/district/category/agency.
          ○​ Per-project drill-down: full reason breakdown, similar/duplicate project links,
              vendor graph view, historical trend chart.
          ○​ Case management: mark as "under review," "confirmed," "false positive" —
              and feed confirmed/false-positive labels back into the model as weak
              supervision to improve future scoring (mention this feedback loop explicitly —
              judges love a model that improves from human review).
          ○​ Export to PDF/CSV for use in actual CAG/ministry review workflows.

Visualization stack suggestion: choropleth + drill-down works well with Mapbox/Leaflet + D3
for custom visuals, or Metabase/Superset embedded panels if you want to save build time
on standard charts and spend your hackathon hours on the detection engine instead — the
detection engine is what should differentiate you, not the charting library.

12. Build Roadmap
Pre-hackathon (do this before the event, it's what separates top-8 finishes from the
rest):

   ●​ Pre-download and clean 2–3 states' worth of real Dataful/data.gov.in MPLADS
      datasets.
   ●​ Pre-build the ETL schema and a small labeled test set from CAG-documented cases.
   ●​ Have the Benford's Law + z-score baseline anomaly detectors working on real data
      before the clock starts — these are fast to build and de-risk your "do we have
      anything working" moment at hour 6.

During the hackathon (typical 24–36hr window):

   1.​ Hours 0–4: Finalize schema, load real + synthetic demo data, stand up
       Postgres/PostGIS.
   2.​ Hours 4–10: Build financial anomaly engine (Benford + z-score + Isolation Forest)
       and the rule-based compliance checks (fast wins, guaranteed-accurate flags).
   3.​ Hours 10–16: Build duplicate-detection (text embeddings + geo clustering) and
       delay-detection modules.
   4.​ Hours 16–22: Wire up the risk-scoring composite, build the FastAPI layer, start
       frontend dashboard.
   5.​ Hours 22–30: Finish dashboard (map + investigator queue), integrate end-to-end,
       inject and verify demo fraud cases fire correctly.
   6.​ Hours 30–36: Polish, prepare the pitch narrative (lead with the
       CAG-documented-case validation — "our system flags a real, confirmed case at
       87/100"), rehearse the demo.




13. Feasibility & Practical Notes
  ●​ Team composition matters more than the model here. This problem statement
     needs: 1 person owning data/ETL, 1–2 owning the ML/detection modules, 1–2
     owning frontend/dashboard, and someone owning the domain narrative (MPLADS
     rules, CAG cases) for the pitch — the domain-storytelling role is often under-staffed
     and is exactly what makes judges trust the technical work.
  ●​ Data access is the biggest real risk, not the modeling — validate that the
     Dataful/data.gov.in exports actually download cleanly before committing to this
     problem statement for the final round, since government portals occasionally have
     uptime/format issues.
  ●​ Scope discipline: it is very tempting to try to cover all of India, all schemes, all years
     — for a hackathon, a 2–3 state, single-scheme, multi-year demo that works
     end-to-end beats a 28-state design that only has mock data behind it.




14. Why This Wins — Pitch Differentiators to
Emphasize
  1.​ Domain credibility: cite the CAG's own repeated criticism and the specific
      mandatory-compliance rules (SC/ST allocation, eligible-use categories) — shows you
      understood the scheme, not just the buzzwords in the prompt.
  2.​ Explainability-first scoring — investigators can act on it; a black-box score cannot
      survive a real audit review.
  3.​ Validation against real, confirmed fraud cases (from CAG reports) rather than
      only synthetic data — this is the single strongest "trust us" moment in a demo.
  4.​ The vendor/agency graph layer — most competing teams will stop at per-project
      anomaly detection; modeling the execution-side network (District Authority → Agency
      → Vendor) targets the actual documented fraud mechanism, not just the numbers.
  5.​ Dual-audience design (public transparency vs. investigator case-management)
      shows product thinking beyond "we built a model," which is often the gap between a
      technically strong team and a top-3 finish.
